  1. The very first product ever scanned by a barcode reader at a supermarket checkout cost just 67 cents. What was it?

    Show answer

    Chewing gum

    It was a pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum at a supermarket in Troy, Ohio. That pack of gum is now on display at the Smithsonian.

    Source: westporttechmuseum.com

  2. Super Glue was discovered by accident while chemists were trying to make see-through plastic for one part of a gun. Which part?

    Show answer

    The sight

    The chemicals stuck to everything they touched, so the researchers threw the idea out. Years later the same chemist realised that the 'flaw' was a superb product.

    Source: todayifoundout.com

  3. The sticky notes covering office monitors exist because a chemist at the company 3M failed at making what?

    Show answer

    A super-strong glue

    Spencer Silver was trying to make an extra-strong adhesive and ended up with one so weak it could be peeled off and stuck back again. It sat unused for years until a colleague wanted bookmarks that wouldn't fall out of his hymn book.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  4. A Viennese mechanic who made tools for surgeons invented the snow globe by accident. What was he actually trying to improve?

    Show answer

    Operating room lamp

    Erwin Perzy put a water-filled glass globe in front of a light and added tiny particles to boost its glare. The flakes drifted down like snow, and his family in Vienna still makes snow globes over a century later.

    Source: smithsonianmag.com

  5. A grinning selfie taken in an Indonesian jungle in 2011 sparked years of legal fights over who owns the copyright. Who, or what, actually pressed the shutter?

    Show answer

    A macaque monkey

    A crested macaque grabbed a wildlife photographer's camera and snapped itself. US authorities later ruled that a photo taken by an animal can't be copyrighted by anyone.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  6. The lightweight folding baby buggy was invented by a British engineer who had earlier designed the folding landing gear of which famous Second World War fighter plane?

    Show answer

    Spitfire

    Owen Maclaren made his buggy from light aluminium tubing and used the same thinking that folded a fighter's wheels into its wings. He came up with it after watching his daughter wrestle a heavy pram while travelling with his grandchild.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  7. Hat makers in the 1800s were famous for trembling and acting strangely, which gave us the phrase 'mad as a hatter'. Which toxic metal in their workshops was to blame?

    Show answer

    Mercury

    Hatters brushed a mercury solution onto animal fur to make it mat into felt, and years of breathing the fumes poisoned their nervous systems. Doctors even called the tremors 'hatter's shakes'.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  8. Many coins have tiny ridges around their edge. They are not there for grip. What were they originally meant to stop people from doing?

    Show answer

    Shaving metal off them

    When coins were real gold and silver, crooks would trim slivers off the edges and melt them down, and a ridged rim made any clipping instantly visible. Isaac Newton himself championed the idea while running England's Royal Mint.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  9. Japan's bullet trains used to make a loud boom when they shot out of tunnels. Engineers fixed it by copying the beak of a bird that dives from air into water with barely a splash. Which bird?

    Show answer

    Kingfisher

    The kingfisher's long, wedge-shaped beak lets it slice from thin air into dense water almost silently. Copying it made the 500 Series Shinkansen quieter, faster and more energy-efficient.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  10. The clocks on GPS satellites tick slightly faster than clocks on Earth, so engineers correct them using a theory by which famous scientist?

    Show answer

    Albert Einstein

    Relativity makes satellite clocks gain about 38 millionths of a second a day. Left uncorrected, your sat nav would drift off by roughly 10 kilometres every single day.

    Source: iaps.info

  11. Blue jeans feel completely American, but their name comes from the old French name of an Italian port city, where the tough cotton cloth was made. Which city?

    Show answer

    Genoa

    The French called Genoa 'Gênes', and cloth from there became 'jean' in English. As a bonus, 'denim' comes from 'de Nîmes', meaning 'from Nîmes' in France.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  12. Hardly any national flag uses the colour purple. What's the historical reason?

    Show answer

    Purple dye was too expensive

    For centuries purple dye came from thousands of crushed sea snails and cost more than gold, so it was reserved for royalty. Today only a handful of flags, such as Dominica's parrot, show any purple at all.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  13. Early spell-checkers didn't recognise the word 'cooperation' without a hyphen, so they swapped it for a Californian place name, and it slipped into official UN and EU documents. Which town, the home of Apple, was it?

    Show answer

    Cupertino

    The old dictionaries only knew 'co-operation' with a hyphen, and 'Cupertino' was the closest match they had. The mistake was so common that it is now called the 'Cupertino effect'.

    Source: mentalfloss.com

  14. Look at the little pull tab on almost any zipper and you will often find the same three letters stamped on it, the initials of one Japanese company. What are those three letters?

    Show answer

    YKK

    YKK stands for Yoshida Kōgyō Kabushiki-gaisha, roughly 'Yoshida Industries Company', and this one Japanese firm makes about half of all the zippers in the world.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  15. The classic barber's pole has red and white stripes. Centuries ago, barbers did more than cut hair. What did the red stripe originally stand for?

    Show answer

    Blood

    Barbers once doubled as surgeons and performed bloodletting, so the red stood for blood and the white for the bandages. Patients even gripped a pole during the procedure to make their veins stand out.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  16. Which button-up knitted jacket is named after the British earl who led the doomed Charge of the Light Brigade?

    Show answer

    Cardigan

    The 7th Earl of Cardigan and his officers wore knitted wool waistcoats in the Crimean War, and his fame turned his title into a wardrobe staple. The same war gave us the balaclava, named after a battle there.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  17. The mild British exclamation 'blimey' is a worn-down version of a much harsher oath. What did the speaker originally ask God to do to them?

    Show answer

    Blind them

    'Blimey' comes from 'God blind me', so every surprised Londoner is technically cursing their own eyesight. Over time the dangerous part got mumbled away until only a polite little gasp was left.

    Source: en.wiktionary.com

  18. Rembrandt's famous painting 'The Night Watch' got its name because it looks so dark. But what time of day does it actually show?

    Show answer

    Daytime

    The scene is set in daylight. Layers of varnish darkened over the centuries until people assumed it was night, and the nickname stuck.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  19. Under a nightclub's ultraviolet light, a glass of tonic water glows an eerie blue. Which bitter ingredient, once taken to fight malaria, makes it shine?

    Show answer

    Quinine

    Quinine fluoresces naturally under UV light, so any drink made with real tonic water glows blue. The gin was originally added to make the anti-malaria medicine easier to swallow.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  20. Today it describes a self-employed designer or writer, but Walter Scott coined this word for a medieval knight whose weapon could be hired by any lord. What is the word?

    Show answer

    Freelance

    In Ivanhoe, Scott wrote of 'free lances', mercenary knights whose lances were sworn to no master. So every freelancer today is, in spirit, a knight for hire.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org
