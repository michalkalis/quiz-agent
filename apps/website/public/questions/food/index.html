Where dishes come from, how they were invented and what you never knew about them.

  1. Ketchup's name comes from a sauce sold in southern China centuries ago, but that original sauce had no tomatoes in it. What was it made from?

    Show answer

    Fermented fish

    The Hokkien 'kê-tsiap' was a salty fish brine. European traders took the name home, tried versions with mushrooms and walnuts, and Americans only settled on tomato in the 1800s.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  2. Kellogg's corn flakes were not first made as a treat. They were invented as deliberately bland food for the residents of what kind of place?

    Show answer

    A sanitarium

    They were made at the Battle Creek Sanitarium in Michigan. Doctor John Harvey Kellogg believed plain, bland food helped calm the passions of his patients, and his brother later added sugar and turned the flakes into a business.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  3. The man who invented conveyor-belt sushi got the idea in the 1950s by watching a production line in a factory. What was that factory making?

    Show answer

    Beer

    Yoshiaki Shiraishi watched bottles gliding along a conveyor at an Asahi brewery and thought plates of sushi could travel the same way. He opened the first sushi-go-round in Osaka in 1958.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  4. The world's most famous restaurant star ratings are awarded by a guide that was launched in 1900 to sell which product?

    Show answer

    Tyres

    The Michelin brothers gave the guide away free to French motorists, hoping that more driving would wear out more tyres.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  5. An Italian bank famously accepts wheels of which cheese as collateral for loans, ageing them in its own vaults until the debt is repaid?

    Show answer

    Parmigiano-Reggiano

    Credito Emiliano lends to producers against their wheels, which gain value as they age, and sells them off if a loan goes bad.

    Source: edition.cnn.com

  6. Until New Zealand exporters rebranded it in 1959, the kiwifruit was sold under what name?

    Show answer

    Chinese gooseberry

    The plant is native to China, and exporters wanted a friendlier, more New Zealand-sounding name, so they borrowed the national bird.

    Source: nzhistory.govt.nz

  7. Which savoury red drink do airline passengers order far more often in the air than on the ground, partly because engine noise makes it taste better?

    Show answer

    Tomato juice

    Loud cabin noise dulls sweet and salty flavours but boosts umami, the savoury taste tomatoes are packed with. So at 10,000 metres tomato juice tastes richer than it does at home.

    Source: news.cornell.edu

  8. A goatherd in Ethiopia noticed his goats leaping about with energy after chewing red berries from a bush. What drink does this legend claim to explain?

    Show answer

    Coffee

    The legend of Kaldi the goatherd is the traditional origin story of coffee. Ethiopia is the plant's native home, and the goats' 'dancing' supposedly led monks to brew the berries to stay awake during prayers.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  9. When posing for a photo, English speakers say 'cheese' to force a smile. In South Korea, people often say the name of a different food instead. Which one?

    Show answer

    Kimchi

    Both words end in the same 'ee' sound, which stretches the lips into a grin. So Korea's favourite fermented cabbage doubles as a camera cue.

    Source: staradvertiser.com

  10. The ice cream name Häagen-Dazs looks Scandinavian, but it was made up and means nothing at all. In which city was the brand actually born?

    Show answer

    New York

    Häagen-Dazs was invented by a couple in the Bronx, New York. They picked a nonsense name with a Danish look because it sounded old-world and luxurious.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  11. Bananas are very slightly radioactive, thanks to the naturally radioactive isotope of which chemical element they are famously rich in?

    Show answer

    Potassium

    A tiny fraction of natural potassium is the radioactive isotope potassium-40, the effect is so well known that physicists jokingly measure small radiation doses in 'banana equivalent doses'.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  12. Broccoli, cauliflower, kale and Brussels sprouts look nothing alike, yet farmers bred every one of them from the same single wild plant. Which vegetable is that plant the wild form of?

    Show answer

    Cabbage

    They are all one species, wild cabbage. Breeders simply favoured different parts: big flower heads for broccoli and cauliflower, leaves for kale, and side buds for Brussels sprouts.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  13. If you swap the ham in eggs Benedict for spinach, the dish takes its name from which Italian city?

    Show answer

    Florence

    It becomes eggs Florentine. In classic French cooking, 'à la florentine' has long meant 'served with spinach', a nod to Florence.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  14. In the 1950s, a small Irish company called Tayto found a way to add seasoning to crisps in the factory, and launched the world's first flavoured crisp. Which two-ingredient flavour was it?

    Show answer

    Cheese and onion

    Before that, crisps were sold plain, often with a little twist of salt in the bag for you to sprinkle yourself. Tayto's founder, Joe 'Spud' Murphy, changed the snack aisle forever, and companies around the world later licensed his method.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  15. Which nut grows dangling beneath a juicy, pear-shaped fruit and is never sold in its shell, because that shell contains an irritant related to the one in poison ivy?

    Show answer

    Cashew

    Cashew trees are in the same botanical family as poison ivy and mango, and the shell must be roasted or steamed off before the nut can be touched safely.

    Source: scientificamerican.com

  16. The English word 'lord' comes from an old phrase meaning 'keeper of the...' what?

    Show answer

    Loaf

    Old English 'hlāford' meant 'loaf-ward', the man who controlled the household's bread. 'Lady' began as 'hlǣfdīge', the 'loaf-kneader'.

    Source: en.wiktionary.org

  17. Which spice, the most expensive in the world by weight, is picked by hand from the stigmas of a crocus flower?

    Show answer

    Saffron

    Each flower yields just three threads, so it takes roughly 150,000 blooms to make a single kilogram.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  18. A jar of honey can sit in the cupboard for years without going off, because any germ that lands in it quickly loses something it cannot live without. What does the honey pull out of it?

    Show answer

    Water

    Honey is so packed with sugar and so low in water that it pulls moisture out of bacteria and moulds by osmosis and dries them out to death. Jam and salted fish keep for the same reason.

    Source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

  19. The cappuccino is named after the brown hooded robes worn by which order of monks?

    Show answer

    Capuchin friars

    The milky-brown coffee matched the colour of the Capuchins' habits. The capuchin monkey got its name from the same friars, thanks to its brown, hood-like fur.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  20. Orchids are usually grown just to look at, but one of them gives us a flavour found in ice cream, cakes and cola. Which flavour comes from the dried seed pod of an orchid?

    Show answer

    Vanilla

    Vanilla comes from a climbing orchid. Outside its native Mexico, almost every flower has to be pollinated by hand, which is why real vanilla is so expensive.

    Source: britannica.com
