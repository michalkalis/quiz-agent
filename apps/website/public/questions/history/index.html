  1. Which future Roman leader, kidnapped by pirates as a young man, reportedly complained that his ransom was set too low, then had his captors crucified once he was freed?

    Show answer

    Julius Caesar

    Plutarch says the pirates asked for twenty talents and Caesar insisted on fifty, then raised ships after his release, captured the pirates and had them executed as he had promised.

    Source: britannica.com

  2. Which former US president, campaigning for a return to office in 1912, was shot in the chest in Milwaukee and insisted on delivering his speech before going to hospital?

    Show answer

    Theodore Roosevelt

    The bullet was slowed by his folded fifty-page speech and a steel glasses case in his pocket, and he spoke for about ninety minutes with blood spreading across his shirt.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  3. Which scientist, famous for his laws of motion, spent the last three decades of his life running England's Royal Mint and personally hunting down counterfeiters?

    Show answer

    Isaac Newton

    Newton became Warden of the Mint in 1696, went undercover in taverns to gather evidence, and sent the notorious forger William Chaloner to the gallows.

    Source: royalmintmuseum.org.uk

  4. Real Viking warriors never wore horned helmets. Which kind of 19th-century stage show gave them their horns?

    Show answer

    Opera

    Costumes for Wagner's Ring cycle in 1876 put horns on Norse heroes, and the image stuck. Archaeologists have never found a single horned helmet from the Viking Age.

    Source: ingebretsens-blog.com

  5. In the 1850s, Emperor Napoleon III reportedly served his most honoured guests with cutlery made of a rare metal, while lesser guests had to settle for gold. What metal was it?

    Show answer

    Aluminium

    Before the Hall-Héroult electrolysis process was developed in 1886, extracting aluminium from ore was so difficult that it cost more than gold. The peak of the Washington Monument was capped with it as a luxury material in 1884, just before the price collapsed.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  6. During the Second World War, people in occupied Norway pinned a humble office item to their coats as a silent protest. Wearing one could get you arrested. What was it?

    Show answer

    A paper clip

    The paper clip stood for 'holding together' against the occupiers, and the idea of a Norwegian paper-clip inventor made it a source of national pride. In 1989 Norway built a giant paper clip statue near Oslo to honour it.

    Source: gizmodo.com

  7. The first powered vacuum cleaners in London around 1901 were too big to fit inside a house. They stood in the street with long hoses fed through the windows. What pulled them from house to house?

    Show answer

    Horses

    Hubert Cecil Booth's machine, nicknamed 'Puffing Billy', was a horse-drawn, engine-powered monster. Rich households hired it as a service, and it was so noisy that it reportedly frightened passing horses.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  8. In 1952, which world-famous physicist was offered the presidency of Israel and politely turned it down?

    Show answer

    Albert Einstein

    The offer came from Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion after the first president died, and Einstein replied that he lacked the aptitude for dealing with people and official functions.

    Source: britannica.com

  9. Before rubber erasers existed, artists and schoolchildren rubbed out pencil marks with something you would normally find on the kitchen table. What was it?

    Show answer

    Bread

    Soft, slightly damp bread lifted the graphite off the paper. When Joseph Priestley noticed around 1770 that a new tree gum did the job better, the material was named for what it did: 'rubber', because it rubbed out.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  10. When the Mona Lisa vanished from the Louvre in 1911, French police hauled in a young Spanish-born painter living in Paris for questioning as a suspect. Who was he?

    Show answer

    Pablo Picasso

    Picasso was questioned because he had previously bought sculptures stolen from the Louvre; the real thief turned out to be Vincenzo Peruggia, a former Louvre worker who hid the painting in his flat for two years.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  11. In the 1630s, the Dutch Republic experienced history's most famous speculative bubble, when a single rare bulb could briefly cost as much as a grand Amsterdam canal house. Which flower drove the frenzy?

    Show answer

    The tulip

    At the peak of tulip mania, exotic striped varieties like Semper Augustus changed hands for fortunes, until the market collapsed almost overnight in February 1637.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  12. Contrary to legend, the pyramid builders of Giza were not slaves but paid workers. Alongside bread, what drink made up a core part of their daily rations?

    Show answer

    Beer

    Egyptian beer was thick, mildly alcoholic and nutritious, practically liquid bread, and workers received it daily, making it as much food and payment as refreshment.

    Source: aeraweb.org

  13. When a Paris hairdresser invented the bob haircut in 1909, he claimed his inspiration was a medieval French heroine burned at the stake. Who?

    Show answer

    Joan of Arc

    The hairdresser Antoine cited Joan of Arc, making the 15th-century saint an unlikely fashion icon.

    Source: youtube.com

  14. Founded in Kyoto in 1889 to make handmade playing cards, which Japanese company went on to become one of the biggest names in video games?

    Show answer

    Nintendo

    Nintendo spent decades selling hanafuda playing cards, and even tried taxis and instant rice, before striking gold with video games in the 1970s and 80s.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  15. In Britain and its colonies, the day after 2 September 1752 was officially 14 September 1752. Why did eleven days simply disappear from the calendar?

    Show answer

    Switch from Julian to Gregorian calendar

    Britain adopted the Gregorian calendar with the Calendar (New Style) Act 1750, catching up with most of Catholic Europe, which had switched back in 1582. The older Julian calendar counted every fourth year as a leap year without exception, so it drifted about three days every four centuries against the solar year. By 1752 the gap had grown to eleven days, so Parliament ordered them dropped in one jump: people went to bed on 2 September and woke up on 14 September. The same act also moved the start of the legal year from 25 March to 1 January.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  16. Mansa Musa, often called the richest man who ever lived, ruled which West African empire in the fourteenth century?

    Show answer

    Mali

    His 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca gave away so much gold along the way that the metal's price in Cairo reportedly slumped for years afterwards.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  17. Long before it ever touched a tree, the chainsaw was invented in the 1780s by two Scottish doctors to speed up what medical procedure?

    Show answer

    Childbirth

    The first chainsaw was a small hand-cranked chain of teeth used to cut through the pelvic bone when a baby was stuck during delivery, a procedure called symphysiotomy. It replaced a knife and saw, and only much later was the idea scaled up for cutting wood.

    Source: en.wikipedia.org

  18. The world's oldest continuously operating university was founded in the year 859 by a woman named Fatima al-Fihri. In which country is it?

    Show answer

    Morocco

    The University of al-Qarawiyyin in Fez, Morocco, is recognised by UNESCO and Guinness as the oldest continuously operating degree-granting university, predating Bologna by over two centuries.

    Source: guinnessworldrecords.com

  19. The wireless technology that pairs your phone with your car is named after a tenth-century king of which country?

    Show answer

    Denmark

    Harald Bluetooth united Danish tribes, so engineers borrowed his name for a standard meant to unite devices, and the logo merges his runic initials.

    Source: bluetooth.com

  20. Captain James Cook took several tonnes of one fermented food on his voyages to keep his sailors from getting scurvy. What was it?

    Show answer

    Sauerkraut

    Fermented cabbage keeps for months at sea and still has some vitamin C. Cook got his reluctant crew to eat it by serving it at the officers' table first, so the sailors decided it must be a treat.

    Source: hekint.org
