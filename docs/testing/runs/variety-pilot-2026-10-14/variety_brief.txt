Write for ordinary adults on a car trip who want to be delighted, not tested. The feeling to aim for after the answer: "Huh, really? I never thought about that!", or before it: "I can work this out!".

WHAT WORKS (the founder rated these 9-10 out of 10):
- The hidden reason behind an everyday thing the player has seen or done: why climbers wear sunglasses on a grey snowfield (snow reflects UV); why an egg that floats is old; why a banana in a paper bag ripens an avocado (ethylene); the first webcam watched a coffee pot.
- A surprising twist on something everybody knows: LEGO makes more tyres than any tyre company; Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid; an Olympic gold medal is mostly silver; Japan sells over 200 Kit Kat flavours.
- A small puzzle the player can reason out from everyday knowledge: which South American country has no coastline; why you cannot fold paper more than about seven times.
- A fun origin of a familiar word or name: "Dumbledore" is Old English for bumblebee; "mile" comes from a thousand Roman paces.

WHAT FAILS (rated 1-5 out of 10), never write these:
- Years, dates, durations ("in which year...", "how long did the empire last").
- People, places or works an ordinary adult has never heard of (a medieval traveller, a jazz bandleader, a festival director), award winners, film crew.
- Plain recall of a school-textbook staple ("who is the King of Pop", "who refused her bus seat in 1955").
- Comparisons where the obvious guess is right ("who drinks more tea, the British or ...").
- Long stems stacking several clues, or a clue that gives the answer away.
- Random facts nobody has a reason to know or care about (what a microphone recorded in the Mariana Trench, what a candy colouring is made of).
- Abstract comparisons nobody can picture (the density of a black hole).
- Russia as a topic.

Test every question before keeping it: would most adults at a dinner table say "oh, nice one!" when they hear the answer? If not, replace it.

VARIETY AND FUN (the founder found plain fact questions "too quiz-like": every question had the same shape, "here is a setup, what is X?"):
- Every batch must feel like a fun round, not a school test. No two questions in a batch may share a shape. Use shapes like these:
  - Riddle: "What am I?" with two or three playful clues that build to something everyone knows.
  - Which one is real: two absurd-sounding claims, only one of them true.
  - Fact or fiction: one surprising statement the player judges as true or false.
  - Odd one out: three familiar things, one does not belong, for a reason that makes people smile.
  - Finish the line: the missing word of a real, funny sign, rule, name or saying.
  - "What happens if...": a small everyday scenario with a surprising real outcome.
  - A plain question is fine, but at most one per batch.
- Keep multiple-choice shapes (which one is real, fact or fiction, odd one out) to at most one per batch of four; the others must be answerable aloud in a few words.
- Find the fun: absurd but true, comic animal behaviour, odd laws and rules, famous blunders and mix-ups, funny origin stories, products that went wrong, kid logic. Wording may wink and tease, but stays short enough for one listen.
- Wordplay is welcome when it works in the players' own language.
